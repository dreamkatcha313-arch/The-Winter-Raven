FIRST COLLECTION

Therapeutic Methods

Structured psychological and behavioral approaches. Self-guided exercises are educational starting points; they do not replace therapy with a qualified practitioner.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy: a thought record

Evidence: established psychotherapy; this worksheet is one CBT tool.Safety: do not use thought-challenging to dismiss real danger; pause if rumination increases.

CBT explores connections among thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, and actions. Structured CBT has evidence for several conditions; a worksheet alone is not equivalent to a full course of therapy.

What you need: Paper or a private notes app, one manageable current situation, and a few quiet minutes.

  1. Describe the situation factually, without interpretation.
  2. Name the emotion and rate its intensity from 0–10.
  3. Write the automatic thought that appeared.
  4. List evidence that supports the thought and evidence that complicates it.
  5. Write a balanced alternative—not forced positivity.
  6. Re-rate the emotion and choose one small, useful next action.

Practice with one situation at a time. If writing increases rumination, stop and consider working with a therapist. Do not use thought challenging to dismiss genuine danger.

Evidence: CBT is an established psychotherapy; the appropriate form depends on the concern. NIMH psychotherapy overview.

Grounding and sensory orientation

Evidence: limited as a stand-alone intervention; commonly used as a coping skill.Safety: adapt sensory input; stop if distress, dissociation, or dizziness increases.

Grounding is a coping skill that may help some people redirect attention toward the present. It is not a stand-alone treatment and does not work the same way for everyone.

  1. Choose a place where you feel reasonably safe. Keep your eyes open if preferred.
  2. Name where you are and notice the support beneath your body.
  3. Identify five things you can see.
  4. Notice four neutral or comfortable physical sensations.
  5. Listen for three sounds, then notice a scent only if comfortable.
  6. Describe one nearby object by color, shape, and texture.
  7. Ask what would help you feel a little safer in the next few minutes.

Use fewer senses if sensory input is overwhelming. Stop if the exercise increases distress, dizziness, or disconnection.

Further reading: VA National Center for PTSD — PTSD Coach.

Gradual exposure: a professionally guided approach

Evidence: established for specific phobias and selected anxiety conditions.Safety: planned with a trained professional; never use unsafe or coercive exposure.

Exposure-based therapy is used for some anxiety disorders and phobias. It involves planned learning in safe situations—not flooding, coercion, or taking physical risks.

  1. Work with a therapist trained in exposure-based treatment to define a specific learning goal.
  2. Identify the feared situation, real hazards, accessibility needs, and a manageable starting point.
  3. Agree on the safety conditions that must remain in place.
  4. Practice the agreed step while noticing predictions and what actually happens.
  5. Review what was learned and decide collaboratively whether to repeat or adjust.

Do not attempt exposure by approaching unsafe animals, heights, traffic, allergens, or other genuine hazards. Trauma-related and interoceptive exposure require clinical judgment.

Further reading: NHS — Phobia treatment.

Behavioral activation: small steps

Evidence: established psychotherapy approach for depression.Safety: adapt for pain, exhaustion, disability, and worsening symptoms.

Behavioral activation is a structured approach often used for depression. It explores how activity, mood, and avoidance influence one another.

  1. Notice one pattern of withdrawal without judging yourself.
  2. List activities that are meaningful, necessary, connecting, enjoyable, or confidence-building.
  3. Break one activity into a step small enough to begin in 5–15 minutes.
  4. Schedule a realistic time and identify likely barriers.
  5. Afterward, note what you did and how it felt before and after.
  6. Adjust the next step based on what you learned.

Balance enjoyment, connection, and accomplishment. Adapt the plan if activity causes marked exhaustion, pain, or worsening symptoms.

Further reading: NICE — Depression in adults.

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) for OCD

Evidence: well-established psychotherapy for OCD.Safety: plan with an OCD-trained clinician; do not improvise exposure or abruptly remove supports.

ERP is a specialized form of cognitive behavioral therapy. It helps a person approach an obsession-related trigger while choosing not to perform the usual compulsion or neutralizing ritual. The aim is new learning and greater freedom—not forcing distress or proving feared outcomes impossible.

  1. Find a licensed mental-health professional with specific ERP experience.
  2. Together, describe the obsession-compulsion cycle, including rituals, avoidance, reassurance seeking, and mental compulsions.
  3. Build a collaborative hierarchy, beginning with a manageable step. Do not copy another person's hierarchy.
  4. Agree on the exposure, the response to practice resisting, and the support plan before beginning.
  5. Practice with clinical guidance, review what was learned, and adjust collaboratively.
  6. Discuss setbacks without shame; do not respond by creating increasingly intense challenges.

ERP is not flooding, punishment, or a demand to tolerate genuinely unsafe situations. A clinician should adapt it for trauma, disability, medical needs, and co-occurring conditions.

Sources: International OCD Foundation — OCD treatment; NICE — OCD and body dysmorphic disorder guidance.

Trauma-focused therapies: choosing supported care

Evidence: established PTSD psychotherapies include CPT, prolonged exposure, and trauma-focused CBT.Safety: clinician-led; this is not a self-guided trauma-processing exercise.

Trauma-focused therapies help people make sense of traumatic experiences and reduce the ways trauma continues to shape daily life. Different approaches have different methods; the person should understand the options and participate in choosing a pace and plan.

  1. Seek a qualified clinician trained in an evidence-based trauma-focused approach.
  2. Ask which therapy they provide—such as Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT), Prolonged Exposure (PE), or trauma-focused CBT—and what sessions involve.
  3. Discuss current safety, goals, cultural context, access needs, dissociation, and other concerns before treatment begins.
  4. Agree on how progress, distress, and between-session practice will be reviewed.
  5. Use grounding or supportive routines as coping tools when helpful, recognizing they are not substitutes for trauma-focused treatment.
  6. Tell the clinician if the pace feels unsafe or destabilizing; treatment can be reviewed and adapted.

Do not recreate traumatic events, force disclosure, or conduct exposure exercises alone based on a website. The appropriate approach depends on the person and circumstances.

Sources: VA National Center for PTSD — PTSD treatment basics; NICE — PTSD guidance.

Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT)

Evidence: established behavioral treatment for tic disorders; access and response vary.Safety: learn with a trained clinician; never shame, punish, or demand suppression of tics.

CBIT is a structured behavioral treatment that can help some people with Tourette syndrome and other tic disorders. It commonly includes awareness training, a competing response, and changes to situations that make tics more likely or disruptive.

  1. Ask a healthcare professional about referral to a clinician trained in CBIT or habit-reversal therapy.
  2. With the clinician, identify a specific tic to work on and notice its pattern and any premonitory urge, if present.
  3. Learn an individualized competing response that is physically safe and incompatible with that tic.
  4. Practice with coaching in a supportive setting, then discuss how to use it in everyday situations.
  5. Identify environmental stressors or situations that may be adjusted without restricting the person's life.
  6. Review progress and comfort; goals should be collaborative and may focus on distress or impairment rather than eliminating every tic.

Not everyone experiences a clear warning urge, and not every tic needs treatment. Accommodations and acceptance remain important. CBIT is not a discipline strategy.

Sources: CDC — Treatment of Tourette Syndrome; NINDS — Tourette Syndrome.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis (CBTp)

Evidence: evidence-supported adjunctive psychotherapy; outcomes vary and it is not a substitute for coordinated care.Safety: work with a clinician experienced in psychosis; do not argue, ridicule, or reinforce frightening beliefs.

CBTp is a collaborative therapy that helps a person explore distressing experiences, understand their effects, develop coping strategies, and work toward personally meaningful goals. It does not require a therapist to dismiss the person's experience or insist on a particular interpretation.

  1. Discuss the option with a qualified mental-health professional or coordinated-care team.
  2. Agree on goals that matter to the person, such as sleep, distress, confidence, relationships, or daily activities.
  3. Explore experiences and interpretations with curiosity and respect, including context, emotions, and effects on behavior.
  4. Identify coping strategies that feel safe and useful, such as reducing stress, planning supportive activities, or responding differently to distressing thoughts or voices.
  5. Review strategies collaboratively and adapt them to the person's preferences, culture, strengths, and support needs.

This is not an instruction to challenge or confront hallucinations or delusions alone. Sudden confusion, inability to care for basic needs, or immediate danger requires prompt professional or emergency support.

Sources: NICE — Psychosis and schizophrenia in adults; NIMH — Schizophrenia.

SECOND COLLECTION

Mind and Body

Everyday habits and mind–body practices that may support general well-being. They are not cures or substitutes for needed care.

Building a supportive sleep routine

Evidence: general sleep-health support; not a stand-alone treatment for mood episodes.Safety: persistent sleep problems or major sleep changes warrant assessment.

  1. Choose a regular wake time that fits your life.
  2. Get natural daylight during the day when possible.
  3. Create a calming wind-down period before bed.
  4. Reduce stimulating activities near bedtime if they interfere with sleep.
  5. Make the room as dark, quiet, and comfortable as practical.
  6. Track sleep and wake times for a week or two and look for patterns, not perfection.

Persistent insomnia, breathing pauses during sleep, or severe daytime sleepiness deserve medical assessment. Do not drive when dangerously sleepy.

Sources: CDC — About Sleep; NHLBI — Healthy Sleep Habits.

Movement for mental well-being

Evidence: moderate evidence for general physical and mental well-being; effects vary.Safety: adapt to ability and health limits; stop for concerning symptoms.

  1. Choose an accessible activity you can do safely: walking, dancing, chair-based movement, gardening, or gentle cycling.
  2. Begin with a short manageable session—even a few minutes is a start.
  3. Attach it to a practical cue, such as after breakfast or during a break.
  4. Notice energy, mood, pain, and recovery.
  5. Increase duration or frequency gradually only if appropriate.
  6. Plan alternatives for weather, fatigue, disability, or limited space.

Prioritize enjoyment and consistency over intensity. Seek medical advice before changing activity substantially if you have health concerns or restrictions.

Sources: WHO — Physical activity; CDC — Benefits of physical activity.

A beginner's mindfulness practice

Evidence: moderate but variable evidence for some stress and symptom outcomes.Safety: may intensify distress for some; switch to external grounding or stop if needed.

  1. Choose a quiet-enough place and a comfortable seated or standing position.
  2. Choose an anchor, such as the feeling of your feet or ordinary sounds.
  3. Attend to it for a few breaths.
  4. When thoughts or sensations arise, notice that attention has wandered.
  5. Gently return to the anchor without criticizing yourself.
  6. Finish by looking around and noticing the room.

Begin briefly. Walking mindfully or noticing an object can replace breath-focused meditation. Stop or switch to external grounding if meditation increases distress, intrusive memories, anxiety, or dissociation.

Source: NCCIH — Meditation and Mindfulness.

THIRD COLLECTION

Natural and Complementary Approaches

Traditional and complementary practices are considered separately from established psychological therapies. Natural does not automatically mean safe, effective, or appropriate for everyone.

Herbal teas and culinary herbs: a safety-first guide

Evidence: limited and plant-specific; tea findings cannot be inferred from concentrated extracts.Safety: allergies, contamination, and interactions are possible; seek professional advice about medicinal use.

A pleasant tea may be part of a comforting routine, but this does not establish that it treats anxiety, depression, or another diagnosis. Evidence differs by plant, preparation, and product. Findings about concentrated extracts cannot automatically be applied to a cup of tea.

  1. Prefer clearly labeled, single-ingredient, food-grade products from reputable suppliers.
  2. Check the botanical name and plant part where available.
  3. Follow ordinary food or package directions; do not concentrate, extract, or increase amounts to pursue a medical effect.
  4. Prepare packaged tea according to its instructions, using clean equipment and fresh water.
  5. Enjoy it as a beverage or comforting ritual, not a measured psychiatric treatment.

Chamomile has been studied for anxiety, but evidence is preliminary. Herbal products can cause allergies, side effects, contamination, and medicine interactions. Chamomile may interact with some medicines and may trigger allergies in people sensitive to related plants. St. John's wort has important interactions and is not a harmless tea. Kava has been linked to severe liver injury and is not recommended in this archive.

Ask a pharmacist or clinician before medicinal herb use if pregnant, breastfeeding, managing a health condition, or taking any medicine. Do not ingest essential oils or use high-dose extracts based on this guide.

Sources: NCCIH — Herbs at a Glance; NCCIH — Safe Use of Complementary Health Products; NCCIH — Anxiety and Complementary Health Approaches.

Yoga and tai chi: gentle mind–body practice

Evidence: moderate, condition-dependent evidence for selected well-being outcomes.Safety: use adaptations; avoid painful movements and seek qualified instruction when needed.

  1. Choose a beginner-friendly instructor who offers adaptations and clear safety guidance.
  2. Share relevant injuries, balance concerns, pregnancy, or health limitations with the instructor.
  3. Use a clear space, comfortable clothing, and a stable chair or wall for support if needed.
  4. Begin with gentle movements within a comfortable range and breathe naturally.
  5. Pause between movements and notice balance, pain, and fatigue.
  6. Skip movements that cause pain, dizziness, numbness, or breathlessness.

Research suggests possible benefits for some aspects of well-being, but results vary. Avoid extreme positions and forceful manipulation. These practices complement—not replace—needed care.

Sources: NCCIH — Yoga; NCCIH — Tai Chi.

CRITICAL RESEARCH

Understanding medication risks and evidence

Winter Raven does not provide prescription medication or narcotic guidance. This section examines documented adverse effects and limitations as part of informed, balanced health literacy—not as an argument that every medication is harmful or that anyone should start, change, or stop a treatment.

Medicines can cause unwanted effects ranging from mild to serious. Risks differ by medication, dose, duration, age, health conditions, and interactions with other medicines or supplements. Research should distinguish common effects from rare serious events and consider benefits demonstrated for the condition being studied.

  • Adverse effects: FDA explains that unwanted reactions can range from minor symptoms to serious injury, and that individual risk factors and combinations matter.
  • Interactions: prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, herbs, and supplements can interact. “Natural” does not mean interaction-free.
  • Long-term effects: evidence may be stronger for some outcomes and time periods than others. A short-term trial does not answer every long-term question.
  • Discontinuation: some medicines can cause withdrawal or rebound effects if stopped suddenly. This archive does not provide tapering instructions; medication changes belong in a conversation with a qualified prescriber.
  • Research quality: look for systematic reviews, transparent methods, comparison groups, absolute as well as relative risks, and disclosure of uncertainty or conflicts of interest.

Do not stop or change a prescribed medication because of information on this website. Anyone concerned about side effects should discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional. Serious suspected adverse effects can also be reported through FDA MedWatch.

Sources: FDA — Finding and Learning about Side Effects; NIMH — Mental Health Medications; MedlinePlus — Considerations when stopping antidepressants.

A SHARED STANDARD

Evidence, safety, and scope

Every guide should distinguish strong, moderate, limited, and insufficient evidence; explain practical implementation; identify risks and contraindications; and cite reputable sources near the claims they support. Prescription medication and narcotic guidance are outside Winter Raven's scope. Critical research on documented medication risks is provided for balanced health literacy, not self-directed treatment changes. Holistic practices can complement appropriate professional care; they should not be represented as substitutes for necessary assessment or treatment.

General guidance: NCCIH safety guidance · NIMH: finding help.

Educational information only—not diagnosis, individualized treatment, or a substitute for qualified professional care. Stop a practice that causes harm or significant distress. Seek professional advice for persistent, severe, or worsening symptoms.
